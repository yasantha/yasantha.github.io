How to focus working from home with kids

Updated 26 October 2026 · 6 min read

Working from home with children around isn't a focus problem you can solve with willpower. Interruptions are part of the job. A school pickup, a spilled drink or a question that can't wait will happen most days.

The goal isn't to block every interruption. It's to plan work so interruptions do less damage, and to get credit for the focused time you actually manage. Here's an approach that works with real family life.

1. Use shorter sessions than the books suggest

The classic Pomodoro session is 25 minutes. With young children at home, 15 or 20 minutes is often more realistic. A short session you finish feels better, and gets more done, than a long one that's broken three times.

Try this: set sessions to 15–20 minutes during the busiest hours, and save longer sessions for nap time, school hours or after bedtime.

2. Find your protected windows

Most homes have a few predictable quiet times: early morning, nap time, a favourite TV show, a partner's turn to take the kids out. Write them down for a normal weekday. Those windows are where your hardest work goes: reports, planning, anything that needs deep thinking.

Everything else (email, forms, ordering, quick replies) fits into the noisier gaps.

3. Pick one main task a day

When your time is broken into pieces, a long to-do list is discouraging. Choose one main task for the day and give it your first protected window. If that's the only thing you finish, the day was still a success.

4. Plan for interruptions instead of fighting them

  • Keep a "park it" note. When a thought or task pops up mid-session, write it down and carry on.
  • Leave a breadcrumb. Before you get up for an interruption, write one line: "Next: finish slide 4 numbers". Restarting becomes much faster.
  • Count the partial session. If you did 12 minutes before being called away, that's 12 real minutes of work. Don't throw them away.

5. Give household tasks their own sessions

Chores and admin expand to fill the day when they have no boundaries. Treat them like work tasks: a 20-minute session for laundry and tidying, one for bills and forms, one for meal prep. When the session ends, stop. It keeps housework from quietly eating your work time, and it makes progress on the home visible too.

Tagging tasks as Work or Personal helps you see the balance at the end of the week.

6. Make focus visible to the kids

Older children can learn a simple signal: headphones on, or a timer on the table, means "working, ask me when it rings". A visual timer they can see works better than "five more minutes". When the timer rings, give them your full attention for a few minutes. It builds trust that you'll come back.

7. Review honestly, once a week

On Friday, look at what actually happened:

  • How many focused sessions did you complete? Which days were best?
  • Which time of day gave you the most uninterrupted work?
  • How did time split between work and home tasks?

Use the answers to plan next week. If mornings are your best time, protect them. If Wednesdays always fall apart, plan lighter work for Wednesdays.

Be kind to yourself

Some days you'll manage two sessions, and that's fine. Working at home with children means doing two jobs at once. Track what you did, not what you hoped to do, and you'll often find you got more done than it felt like.
