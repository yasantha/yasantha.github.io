Best Pomodoro timer length: 15, 25, 45 or 50 minutes?

Published 11 November 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer: 25 minutes is a good default, but no research shows it is the best length for everyone. Pick the session length that matches the task and your energy, then test it for a week. This guide explains where 25 minutes came from, what the research does and doesn't say, and how to choose between 15, 25, 45 and 50.

Treat these as starting points. The 15-minute advice and the task matching come from practical experience with the method, not from studies that compared these exact lengths.

Where 25 minutes came from

Francesco Cirillo developed the technique in the late 1980s as a university student, working in blocks timed by a tomato-shaped kitchen timer. His standard pattern is a 25-minute session, a 5-minute break, and a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes after four sessions. He also advises not splitting or shortening a session once it has started.

That 25 minutes came from Cirillo's own practice. I couldn't find a controlled study that shows it is the best length, so it is better to see it as a handy, tested-by-use default than as a scientific optimum.

What the research says about work blocks and breaks

Brief breaks can protect focus on long tasks

In a 2011 study in the journal Cognition, researchers at the University of Illinois asked 84 people to do a repetitive computer task for about 50 minutes. Most people's performance dropped over time. A group that was briefly pulled away from the main task twice kept performing steadily. The researchers' reading is that briefly stepping away and then returning keeps your goal active. This was a lab task, so it doesn't prove the same effect for studying, but it supports taking regular breaks on long jobs.

Short breaks help how you feel, and longer breaks may help more for hard work

A 2022 meta-analysis in PLOS ONE pooled 22 studies of "micro-breaks", meaning pauses of ten minutes or less. Micro-breaks were linked to more energy and less fatigue. They were not linked to better task performance overall. Longer breaks did tend to go with better performance, mostly on clerical and creative tasks and less so on demanding thinking. For heavy mental work, that is a reason to be generous with your break rather than squeezing it to the minimum.

Your attention doesn't switch off at a fixed minute

You'll often hear that attention fades after 10 to 15 minutes. A 2016 editorial in Advances in Physiology Education reviewed the original data behind that claim in lectures and concluded that it doesn't support a fixed limit. Differences between sessions came more from the teacher than from the clock. So there is no biological timer that makes 25 minutes magic, and no one number you must obey.

The famous 52/17 rule is a data point, not a law

In 2014 the time-tracking company DeskTime reported that its most productive users tended to work for about 52 minutes and then rest for about 17. The data came from the company's own software, with productivity judged by the share of computer time spent in apps seen as productive, and the full sample details weren't published. When DeskTime repeated the analysis in 2021 it reported about 112 minutes of work and 26 of rest, which it linked to remote working, and it noted that the pre-pandemic figure from early 2020 was closer to 80 and 17. The numbers moved a lot, which is the real lesson: the best rhythm depends on your situation.

How to choose your length

  • Choose 15 minutes when you're avoiding a task, tired, or having a bad day. The goal is to start, and it's easy to keep going if it's going well.
  • Choose 25 minutes for tasks you can split into small pieces: flashcards, reading, practice questions and admin.
  • Choose 45 to 50 minutes for work that needs a long warm-up, like essays, coding or a full past paper, and take a 10-minute break afterwards.

Two signs your sessions are the wrong length. If you keep stopping in the middle of a thought, they're too short. If the last ten minutes are mostly staring and checking the clock, they're too long.

A one-week test to find your length

  1. Days 1–2: use 25 minutes with 5-minute breaks.
  2. Days 3–4: use 45 minutes with 10-minute breaks on your hardest task.
  3. Day 5: use 15 minutes on the task you've been putting off.
  4. After each session, rate your focus from 1 to 5 and note whether you drifted, stopped early or wanted to keep going.
  5. At the end of the week, keep the length that gave you the highest scores for the kind of work you do most.

Your best length may differ by subject, so it's fine to use two. Our guide to using the Pomodoro technique for studying shows how to plan a whole revision day around them.

Common questions

Is 25 minutes scientifically proven to be best?

No. It's Cirillo's practical choice and it works well for many people, but the research above doesn't point to one best length.

What if I'm in the middle of something when the timer ends?

Finish the sentence or the problem you're on, then take your break. A short extension is fine, as long as you still take the break and don't turn it into a habit of skipping them.

Should my break always be 5 minutes?

Five minutes is a good default after a 25-minute session. After 45 to 50 minutes, 10 minutes is a more realistic reset. See our guide to focusing when you get interrupted at home for what to do when a break gets taken for you.
