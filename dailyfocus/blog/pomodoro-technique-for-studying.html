How to use the Pomodoro technique for studying

Updated 26 October 2026 · 7 min read

The Pomodoro technique is one of the simplest ways to start studying when you don't feel like it. You pick one task, work on it for a fixed block of time, take a short break, and repeat. Francesco Cirillo created the method as a university student, using a tomato-shaped kitchen timer (pomodoro is Italian for tomato).

It works for studying because it turns "revise biology" (vague and endless) into "one 25-minute session on cell transport" (small and finishable). This guide shows how to set it up, how to plan a revision day with it, and the mistakes that make people give up.

The basic Pomodoro cycle

  1. Pick one task. Make it specific: "Chapter 4 practice questions 1–10", not "maths".
  2. Work for one session. The classic length is 25 minutes. Don't switch tasks or check your phone.
  3. Take a short break. About 5 minutes. Stand up, drink water, look away from screens.
  4. Repeat. After four sessions, take a longer break of 15–30 minutes.

That's all there is to it. The power comes from the rules you keep: one task per session, and a real break afterwards.

How long should a study session be?

25 minutes is a starting point, not a law. Adjust it to the kind of work and to how you feel on the day:

SessionGood forBreak
15 minutesGetting started on a subject you're avoiding, or a tired evening3–5 min
25 minutesFlashcards, reading, practice questions5 min
45–50 minutesEssays, long maths problems, coding, past papers10 min

If you keep stopping halfway through a thought, your sessions are too short. If you're staring at the page for the last ten minutes, they're too long.

Plan your subjects before you start

Most lost study time goes on deciding what to do next. Before your first session, write down the tasks for the day and how many sessions each one deserves. A realistic revision day for a student with exams might look like this:

  • Maths: past paper section B, 3 sessions
  • Biology: cell transport flashcards, 2 sessions
  • English: essay plan for Macbeth, 2 sessions
  • Catch-up: anything missed earlier in the week, 1 session

Eight sessions of 25 minutes is about three and a half hours of real, focused study. That's more than many students manage in a full day of "studying" with their phone next to them.

Pick your hardest subject for the first two sessions, while your energy is highest.

What to do in the breaks

A break should give your attention a rest. Scrolling social media usually doesn't, because it pulls you into another stream of information and makes it hard to come back. Better options:

  • Stretch, walk to the kitchen, refill your water
  • Look out of a window for a minute
  • Tidy your desk for the next session
  • Say out loud one thing you just learned. It's a quick bit of active recall.

When you get interrupted

Interruptions happen: a sibling, a parent, a message you have to answer. Two simple rules help:

  • Short interruption: note it on paper ("reply to Sam"), then deal with it in your break.
  • Real interruption: stop the session, but keep credit for the minutes you did. Ten honest minutes still count.

Track sessions, not hours

"I studied all afternoon" often means two hours of real work and two of drifting. Counting completed sessions is more honest and more motivating. Seeing eight sessions done feels like real progress, and over a week you can spot which subjects you keep avoiding.

At the end of each week, ask three questions:

  1. Which subject got the most sessions? Is that the one that needed them?
  2. What time of day did I focus best?
  3. Which tasks did I keep moving to tomorrow?

Common mistakes

  • Skipping breaks. It feels productive, but focus drops off and the next session is harder to start.
  • Tasks that are too big. "Revise chemistry" never feels finished. "Balance 10 equations" does.
  • Phone on the desk. Put it face down across the room, or use it only for the timer.
  • Giving up after one bad day. Some days you'll manage three sessions, not eight. Three is still three more than zero.

A quick start for today

Choose one task you've been putting off. Set a 25-minute timer. Work on only that task until it rings, then take five minutes away from your desk. That's your first Pomodoro, and the hardest one is now behind you.
