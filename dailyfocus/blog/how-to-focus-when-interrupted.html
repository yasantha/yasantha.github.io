How to focus when you keep getting interrupted

Published 11 November 2026 · 6 min read

You can't remove every interruption: a colleague, a child, a notification, your own wandering mind. What you can do is make interruptions cheaper. This guide explains why they cost so much and gives you a simple three-part routine: decide in advance, park the thought, and make it easy to get back.

Why interruptions cost more than the seconds they take

Research by Gloria Mark and colleagues at the University of California, Irvine, found that interrupted work tends to get done faster, but at a price. In a lab study, people who were interrupted reported more stress, mental workload, frustration, time pressure and effort. In a workplace study of 36 people across three organisations, getting back to the original task after an interruption took an average of about 23 minutes, partly because people worked on other things in between. Not every interruption costs 23 minutes, and the sample was small, but the point stands: the real cost is the return trip.

Sophie Leroy, a researcher at the University of Washington Bothell, calls one reason for this attention residue: part of your attention stays with the earlier task instead of moving fully to the next one. It is more likely when you leave something unfinished, are interrupted, or expect to have to rush through pending work later.

Step 1: Decide what counts as an interruption, in advance

Most of the damage happens in the second after the interruption, when you have to decide what to do. Make that decision ahead of time with a simple if-then rule. Researchers call these "implementation intentions". A review of 94 studies found that if-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals, and that they helped most where distractions and forgetting were the problem. They only work when the plan is specific, so write yours as a clear sentence:

  • If someone asks a quick question during a session, then I'll say "Give me until 10:30" and note it down.
  • If a message pops up, then I'll leave it until my break.
  • If I get the urge to check my phone, then I'll write "phone" on paper and take one slow breath.
  • If it's urgent (a call from school, a real emergency), then I'll stop the session without guilt.

Step 2: Park the thought on paper

Unfinished tasks tend to keep pulling at your attention. In a 2011 study in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, people who were left with unfinished goals had more intrusive thoughts and did worse on an unrelated task. When they made a specific plan for those goals, the effect largely disappeared, even though the goals weren't finished yet.

You can use this with a "parking note": keep a sheet of paper next to you. When something pops into your head, write one line and a time, for example "reply to Sam, after the break". It takes five seconds, and you've given the thought a plan, so it has less reason to keep nagging you.

Step 3: Leave yourself a way back in

Before you step away, whether you chose to or not, write one line on where you were and what you'll do next: "Question 6, check the sign in line 3". When you return, you read the line and start. You don't need to rebuild the whole picture first.

  • Restart small. Use a short 15-minute session to get back in. See our guide to the best Pomodoro timer length for choosing between 15, 25 and 45 minutes.
  • Keep credit for the time you did. If you worked for 12 minutes before being called away, that's 12 real minutes.
  • Finish a sentence before you leave. A half-written thought is harder to pick up again than a finished one.

Make interruptions easier to say no to

  • Make your focus time visible. Headphones, a closed door or a timer on the desk tell people you're busy better than asking them not to disturb you. At home, a visual timer works well for children.
  • Batch the small stuff. Check messages and answer quick requests in your breaks, not between them.
  • Remove what you control. Silence notifications on your phone and computer for the length of a session. Most self-interruptions start with a buzz.
  • Shorten sessions when the day is chaotic. At home with children, 15 to 20 minutes you finish beats 45 minutes that get broken. Our guide to focusing while working from home with kids has a full plan.

A routine you can use today

  1. Write two or three if-then rules for the interruptions you get most.
  2. Put a sheet of paper next to your keyboard for parking notes.
  3. Before each session, write your first step. Before you stop, write your next one.
  4. When an interruption comes, follow your rule, park the thought and get back in with a short session.

Common questions

Does every interruption really cost 23 minutes?

No. That figure is an average from one workplace study, and it includes time spent on other tasks before returning. A quick interruption you handle with a plan costs far less than one that pulls you into something else.

Should I restart my session after an interruption?

If you were near the end, finish the task and then take a break. If it was early, start a fresh short session and write down your restart line first. Either way, count the minutes you really worked.
